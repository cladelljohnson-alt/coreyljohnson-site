6 YA Books with Black Protagonists About Mystery, Truth, and Identity

Not every book here is a traditional mystery. I chose these because they put Black young people at the center of questions about truth, justice, identity, or what happens when adults decide not to look closer. I’ve labeled the reading mood so you can pick what fits.

If you came specifically for a missing-person mystery, start with Monday’s Not Coming. The other books take different routes into suspense, social questions, and identity.

Mystery and psychological suspense

Allegedly by Tiffany D. Jackson — Psychological suspense about a teenager convicted in the death of a child. The story keeps you questioning the account of what happened and who gets believed.

Monday’s Not Coming by Tiffany D. Jackson — Missing-person suspense. Claudia returns from summer break to find that her best friend Monday has vanished, and the adults around her aren’t responding with the urgency Claudia expects.

Social suspense and hard questions

Dear Martin by Nic Stone — Contemporary YA fiction about a Black teenager trying to make sense of race, justice, and the way people see him. It isn’t a whodunit; the tension comes from the choices and systems the characters face.

All American Boys by Jason Reynolds and Brendan Kiely — Contemporary fiction told through two teens’ perspectives after one of them is assaulted by a police officer. The book asks what responsibility looks like when people witness harm.

Identity and supernatural adventure

The Poet X by Elizabeth Acevedo — A novel in verse about Xiomara, a teenager finding her voice while navigating family expectations and her own beliefs. This is coming-of-age fiction, not a mystery.

Ghost Squad by Claribel Ortega — A supernatural adventure for younger YA readers, following Lucely and her friend Syd as they try to undo a spell that threatens their town.

When a feeling becomes a clue

A character’s instinct can be a useful first nudge in a thriller. It shouldn’t have to do all the work. The story still needs observations, questions, and evidence that help the character—and reader—decide what to believe.

Representation belongs in every genre

These books don’t all tell the same kind of story, and they don’t need to. Their Black protagonists get to investigate, question, grieve, joke, grow, and change. If you want to hear more of my perspective on that, read why representation matters in YA thrillers.
